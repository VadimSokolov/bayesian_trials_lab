Optional: how the gap score works (you can skip this)

Walk around the circle and measure each gap between neighboring points, as a share of the whole circle. Square each gap, add them up, and multiply by the number of points: \(s = T \sum (\text{gap}/L)^2\), with \(T = 15\) points and \(L = 100\) the circle's length.

Perfectly even points have every gap equal to 1/15, so \(s = 1\). All points at one spot leave one gap equal to the whole circle, so \(s = 15\).

Random points score \(2T/(T+1) = 1.875\) on average, and most land between 1.4 and 2.6.

Randomization

In physics, if you repeat an experiment the same way, you get almost the same result. So if you change one thing and the result changes, that change caused it.

Patients are not like that. Two patients on the same drug can do very differently, because of health, age, habits and things we cannot control.

Sicker patients tend to do worse no matter what they take. If more of them end up on placebo, the placebo group does worse because of who is in it, and the drug looks better even if it does nothing.

So we flip a coin for each patient: heads gets the drug, tails gets placebo. Then healthy and sick patients end up in both groups in about the same numbers. The coin also splits things we never measured, like sleep or diet. What is left is luck, and the randomization test below shows how big luck can be.

The randomization test

In a trial, a coin flip decides who gets the drug. Suppose the drug does nothing, so each patient's result stays fixed. Re-randomizing flips the coins again and asks how big a gap luck makes.

Takeaway

  • Chance makes differences too.
  • A test asks: is the gap bigger than chance usually makes?
  • Small trials fool everyone. Big trials let real gaps stand out.

This is why trials randomize: the coin flip itself tells us what gaps chance makes. The share of re-randomizations with a gap at least as big as the real one is the p-value.
