Module 8 · Bet on the drug

Peek without penalty

Can you look at your trial after every patient and still not cheat?

Try an example:

1. The bet

Start with $1 and bet part of it on each pair of patients (one on drug, one on placebo). If only the drug patient improves you win the bet, if only the placebo patient improves you lose it.

2. Add patients

40 simulated trials. A trial that reaches $40 stops and declares the drug works. Your money at any moment is the e-value. Each step up the axis is 10 times more money.

Why a useless drug rarely wins

If the drug does nothing, each bet is fair, so the money does not grow on average. Ville (1939) proved it reaches $40 in at most 1 trial in 40 (2.5%), however often you look. So a useless drug rarely wins.

A Bayes factor (how much better one story fits the data than another) against a simple “no effect” null is itself an e-value.

The price: power

If you fix the check times in advance, a planned-checks test finds a working drug more often. The e-value trades some of that for checking any time.

Power = chance a working drug (45% vs 30%, 200 per arm) reaches the bar.

Regulator's corner

ICH E20 is a draft international guideline (Step 2, 2025) for trials that change their plan midway. It says:

"controlling the chances of false positive efficacy conclusions" is "an essential element of regulatory decision-making"

ICH E20 draft (Step 2, June 2025), section 3.3. E-values give this at every look, but regulators rarely use them yet.

Takeaway

  • Evidence is wealth from fair bets: if the drug does nothing, it does not grow on average, so it rarely reaches $40 (at most 1 time in 40).
  • You may peek after every patient. Usual p-values cry wolf far more than 2.5% of the time.
  • The price: with planned check times, a planned test finds a working drug more often.
