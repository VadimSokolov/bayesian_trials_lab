Choosing the sample size

Before a trial starts, the team picks the smallest gain worth catching, the false-alarm bar, and the power they want. It also estimates how much patients vary. The only thing left to choose is the number of patients.

A shortcut

\[ n \approx \frac{(z_{\alpha} + z_{\beta})^2\,\big[p_0(1-p_0) + p_1(1-p_1)\big]}{(p_1-p_0)^2} \]

n is patients per group. \(p_0\) and \(p_1\) are the old and new improvement rates. \(z_\alpha = 1.96\) for the bar. \(z_\beta = 0.84\) for 80% power, 1.28 for 90%. The top is how much patients vary. The bottom is the gain, squared. It lands within a few patients of the curve.

More in Clinical Trials in Practice, chapter 11.

Halve the gain and you need about 4 times the patients. Going from 80% to 90% power needs about a third more.

Regulator's corner

The ICH E20 draft describes a false alarm as "conclusions that truly inefficacious treatments are efficacious" (useless drugs called useful). That is our α, the Type I error.

ICH E20, section 3.3 (draft, June 2025, not final). Read the guideline (FDA).

Takeaway

  • A trial can raise a false alarm (Type I, α) or miss a drug that works (Type II, β). Power is 1 minus β.
  • More patients help detect a working drug without raising the false-alarm rate. Moving the bar trades one error against the other.
  • Half the effect costs about four times the patients.
