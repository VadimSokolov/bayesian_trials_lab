A real case: star signs and aspirin

In 1988, the ISIS-2 trial gave aspirin or placebo to 17,187 patients with a suspected heart attack. Aspirin clearly helped overall. To warn about slicing the data, its authors split patients by star sign. Aspirin seemed to fail for two signs, Gemini and Libra.

Twelve signs, twelve chances for luck. Below: a drug that really helps every sign equally, split by sign. Some bars still fall short.

Bars are simulated, not ISIS-2 data. The drug works equally in all 12 signs.

Another one: a site that looks bad

A drug helps 30% of patients at every site. One site enrolls 19 patients and only 1 improves. Look at card 5 above for how often luck alone does that when the drug is tested at many sites.

The page's own binomial formula gives the 1-in-100 number. We treat each site as separate luck.

Regulator's corner

The trial rulebook ICH E9 (section 5.6) lists where many tests come from: several main outcomes, several treatments, and repeated looks at the data before the trial ends.

Write the fix into the trial plan before any results come in. ICH E9, Statistical Principles for Clinical Trials (PDF)

Takeaway

  • Every extra test is another ticket in the false alarm lottery.
  • A fix (Bonferroni or a fixed order) pays for the extra tickets so the chance of any false alarm stays near 2.5%.
  • The percentages here assume independent tests. Bonferroni keeps the chance of any false alarm at or below 2.5% even when tests are related.
  • Decide the tests and their order before you see the data.
