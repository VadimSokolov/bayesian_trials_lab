Real cases: when "it helps" was wrong

Thalidomide (1950s to 1962). About 10,000 babies were born with birth defects, mostly outside the US, where FDA's Frances Kelsey had refused it.

Congress then required proof that drugs work (1962), with adequate and well-controlled trials.

CAST (late 1980s). Heart drugs fixed a rhythm marker on a heart monitor, but more patients died than on placebo. The trial stopped early.

Sources: Sokolova and Sokolov (2026), Clinical Trials in Practice, chapters 1 (history), 17 (endpoints) and 16 (randomization); Rossouw et al., Women's Health Initiative, JAMA 2002; CAST Investigators, N Engl J Med 1989.

Regulator's corner

"Substantial evidence" is the law's phrase for this page's question: adequate, well-controlled trials. FDA's revised draft guidance (June 2026; it revises the December 2019 draft and will replace the 1998 guidance) (section IV.A) describes one adequate, well-controlled trial plus confirmatory evidence. The evidence can come from a highly persuasive trial or from strong confirmatory evidence:

"a highly persuasive trial or a source of strong confirmatory evidence"

FDA draft guidance, Demonstrating Substantial Evidence of Effectiveness for Human Drug and Biological Products. Revised draft. FDA also weighs trial quality and what the result means for patients.

Takeaway

  • If the true effect equals the first estimate, an equally large repeat trial barely wins about half the time.
  • Two wins by luck are rare (1 in 1,600). One very strong win plus other evidence can also count.
  • A fast review is not a weaker standard. A marker that improves is not proof that patients do better.
