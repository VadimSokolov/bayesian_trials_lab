Adaptive and Bayesian designs

Can a trial change its plan on the way and still be fair?

Bayesian means updating a belief with each patient, as in module 1.

Power is the chance a trial finds a real effect (module 3). A false positive (a false win) calls a useless drug a success. The target is 2.5% (the one-sided 0.025 line from module 2). The default bar 0.90 gives about 10% false wins. A bar of 0.975 ("97.5% sure") gives about 2.5%, but only with no prior opinion and one look at the data.

Truth versus what the trial sees

The true response rates are hidden from the trial. Patients arrive one by one. The better a treatment looks so far, the likelier the next patient gets it. This is response-adaptive randomization: more patients go to the treatment doing better.

Benefit: more patients get the better treatment (watch "On the drug"). Pitfall: early luck can fool it (try "Start over with new luck"), and it can need more patients for the same power.

Regulator's corner

The FDA's draft guidance on Bayesian methods (January 2026) describes a success rule like ours as:

"the posterior probability that the true treatment effect size exceeds some threshold"

Posterior probability = the chance after the trial. Effect size = the gap between the groups.

FDA draft guidance on Bayesian methods, section IV.A. The Bayesian guidance is still a draft. Adaptive plans: FDA guidance (2019).

Takeaway

  • Before the trial, simulate a Bayesian rule to count its false positives and its power.
  • Borrowing history helps when it matches today and hurts when it does not.
  • A trial may adapt, if the rules are written before the first patient.
