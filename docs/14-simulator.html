Trial simulator: test the rule before you trust it

A trial may stop early when the trial records look great. How often is that a false alarm?

Each trial has two groups (arms): drug and placebo. A look is a pause to check the trial records. A false positive is a trial that calls a useless drug a success.

3. The stopping rule

This lab starts every arm from no opinion, a flat prior. At each look, find the chance the drug is better. Above the success bar: stop and say success.

Below it at an early look: stop and give up. Off = never give up.

Fixed and classic designs always use equal groups.

Fixed: one look, at the end. Classic rule: looks set so a useless drug wins about 2.5% under the bell-curve approximation. The simulated false-win rate in the table is the number to trust. Your bar: the rule set on the left.

Find a fair bar

Calibrate finds the lowest success bar at which a useless drug wins 2.5% of trials or fewer (the one-sided 0.025 line, counting only wins for the drug). It checks one placebo rate only. A real design checks the false-win rate at every plausible placebo rate.

It tests bars on one batch of simulated trials, then checks the best one on a new batch.

Predict, then check

A real trial that set its bar by simulation

The Pfizer-BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine trial (2020) used a Bayesian success rule: the chance that the vaccine cuts COVID cases by more than 30% had to pass 98.6% at the final analysis.

“to compensate for the interim analysis and to control the overall type 1 error rate at 2.5%”

(Its looks would otherwise raise false positives above 2.5%.)

The bar is above 97.5% for the same reason as above. The final count was 8 cases among vaccinated people and 162 on placebo (95.0% efficacy).

Source: Polack et al. (2020), doi:10.1056/NEJMoa2034577 (full text).

Regulator’s corner

FDA guidance (2019) and its Bayesian draft (2026) ask sponsors of complex or Bayesian adaptive designs, where formulas do not give the error rate, to show by simulation that false positives stay in check. The draft international guideline ICH E20 (2025) agrees.

Takeaway

  • More looks mean more chances for luck to cross a fixed bar, so the bar must rise.
  • Simulation finds the bar: run the do-nothing drug thousands of times and count.
  • Trials that stop early on good news tend to overstate the drug's benefit, on average.
