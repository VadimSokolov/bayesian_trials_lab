Module 10

Platform trials

Ten new drugs to test. Do you run ten trials, or one that keeps running?

Three kinds of master protocol

A master protocol runs many sub-studies under one roof.

Steer patients to the better drug

Adaptive: more new patients go to the drug doing better so far. Will more patients get the better drug?

The lab's rule is Bayesian (Thompson-style): treatments with a higher chance of being the best, given the data so far, get more patients.

The cost: this can cut power (module 3). A drift in the response rate over time can also raise false positives.

Real example 1: I-SPY 2

A breast cancer platform that ran from 2010 to 2022. An arm graduates when its chance of winning a later trial reaches 85%.

Source: Sokolova, Sokolov and Polson (2026) and the platform trial chapters of Clinical Trials in Practice.

Real example 2: STAMPEDE

A UK prostate cancer platform shared one control arm across 10 other arms, and abiraterone cut the death rate by about a third.

Sources: STAMPEDE final recruitment figures; abiraterone result, James et al. (2017), as cited in Clinical Trials in Practice.

Regulator's corner

The FDA's revised draft guidance on master protocols (June 2026) says some master protocols may:

"improve efficiency by using a shared control arm in evaluating multiple drugs"

FDA draft guidance, Master Protocols for Drug and Biological Product Development. Revised draft (June 2026). In the lab: fewer control patients per drug.

Takeaway

  • One shared control group saves patients: 480 instead of 800 for five drugs.
  • Steering patients to the better drug helps them, but one trial can steer the wrong way.
  • Compare each drug with controls from the same time.
