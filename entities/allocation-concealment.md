## What it is
Sequence generation produces the unpredictable list. Allocation concealment keeps that list hidden from the people who decide whether, and when, a patient enters the trial, until the patient is irreversibly enrolled. [sec-randomization-concealment]

## Why it matters
Concealment is about the time before the assignment. [[blinding]] is about the time after it. A trial can be concealed and open label, which prevents selection bias but not bias in treatment and assessment. A trial cannot be properly blinded without concealment. [sec-randomization-concealment]

## In the book
The practical means are central randomization by an interactive response system, sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelopes and pharmacy-controlled coded packs. Central telephone or web randomization is the strongest and the usual choice in pivotal trials. [sec-randomization-concealment]

Schulz and colleagues examined 250 controlled trials. Inadequately concealed allocation went with odds ratios exaggerated by about 41 percent, and unclear concealment by about 30 percent. These are averages over one body of trials, an association and not a randomized comparison. [sec-randomization-failures] Another paper describes clinicians holding envelopes up to bright lights and opening several at once. [sec-randomization-failures]

The site sees only the kit number, returned after the patient is irreversibly enrolled. [5/how-the-schedule-is-actually-made] The protocol should say how concealment was done, and the book gives a model paragraph. [sec-randomization-concealment]

## Pitfalls
Even a perfectly random list is useless if the enrolling clinician can read it. [sec-randomization-failures]

## Check yourself
- Q: Can a trial be concealed but open label? A: Yes. Investigator selection bias is prevented, but bias in treatment and assessment is not. [sec-randomization-concealment]
- Q: Which method is strongest? A: Central telephone or web randomization, because it cannot be opened in advance. [sec-randomization-concealment]
