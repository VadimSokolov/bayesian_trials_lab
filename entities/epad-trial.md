## What it is

The European Prevention of Alzheimer's Dementia program (EPAD) is the standard example of a platform that did not work. It was a 64-million-euro, five-year IMI-funded project that built a 2,000-participant readiness cohort with pre-characterization such as amyloid status, cognitive baselines and longitudinal follow-up. [20/epad-a-platform-that-never-opened-an-arm]

## Why it matters

The cohort was meant to reduce the high screen-failure rates of Alzheimer's prevention trials. It never enrolled a single treatment arm. [20/epad-a-platform-that-never-opened-an-arm] In the project's post-mortem, two obstacles were decisive: owners of candidate interventions were reluctant to hand sponsorship of a critical asset to a university-run platform, and stakeholders could not agree on a legal framework. [20/epad-a-platform-that-never-opened-an-arm]

## In the book

When the five-year term ended in 2020 the treatment platform had never opened, and the pandemic had made funding harder. The cohort data and biobank outlived the project. [20/epad-a-platform-that-never-opened-an-arm] The lesson is organizational and not statistical: nothing in the statistical design was at fault, and the sample-size, borrowing and decision rules were never tested. [20/epad-a-platform-that-never-opened-an-arm]

## Pitfalls

Platforms need three things before a first arm: a legal framework arm sponsors will sign, a funding model that does not depend on the commercial pipeline, and governance that answers who decides. The Healey ALS platform is the contrast. [20/epad-a-platform-that-never-opened-an-arm]
