## What it is
The book writes $p_{12}$, $p_{23}$ and $p_{3A}$ for the probabilities of moving from Phase I to II, from II to III, and from III to approval. [sec-lifecycle-options]

## Why it matters
Each transition probability enters the cost per approved drug. Programs that use biomarkers to select patients have higher success probabilities. [sec-lifecycle-options] A Phase II predictive distribution gives the probability that Phase III will succeed. [sec-lifecycle-reading]

## In the book
Wong, Siah and Lo used over 400,000 trial records for more than 21,000 compounds and estimated that about 13.8 percent of programs entering Phase I eventually reach approval, and 3.4 percent in oncology. [sec-lifecycle-options] Over 2011 to 2020 the likelihood that a drug entering phase I was approved was 7.9 percent, and only 28.9 percent of candidates cleared phase II. [1/the-price-of-a-trial-of-being-wrong-and-of-waiting] Of 43 phase III studies that repeated the regimen of a preceding phase II study, only 12 (28 percent) were positive. [1/the-price-of-a-trial-of-being-wrong-and-of-waiting]

## Pitfalls
Raising a transition probability by advancing more compounds is not better selection. The case for predictive methods is that they advance the right ones. [sec-lifecycle-options]

## Check yourself
- Q: Do the published approval rates agree to the decimal? A: No. The book quotes 13.8 percent from one source and 7.9 percent from another, for different data and periods. [sec-lifecycle-options] [1/the-price-of-a-trial-of-being-wrong-and-of-waiting]
