## What it is
A new medicine reaches patients through a sequence of experiments, each larger, longer and more expensive than the last, and each built to answer a different question. [sec-lifecycle] A compound enters human testing after discovery, lead optimization and preclinical studies, and in the United States the sponsor then files an Investigational New Drug application. [sec-lifecycle-pre] The book treats each phase as an experiment whose value lies in the decision it informs: whether to proceed, at what dose, in which patients. [sec-lifecycle]

## Why it matters
Most drugs fail along the way. Over 2011 to 2020 the likelihood that a drug entering phase I was eventually approved was 7.9 percent, and phase I to approval took on average 10.5 years. [1/the-price-of-a-trial-of-being-wrong-and-of-waiting]

## In the book
The phases have regulatory definitions but need not form a rigid sequence. 21 CFR 312.21 describes Phases 1 to 3 and expressly allows them to overlap. [sec-lifecycle] ICH E8(R1) (Step 4, October 2021) classifies studies by objective and says the classic phase labels do not fit every program. [sec-lifecycle] ICH E9 distinguishes exploratory from confirmatory trials, and the type I error discipline of the book applies to the confirmatory stage. [sec-lifecycle] The prior for everything that follows is built before Phase I, from animal data, mechanistic models and the history of similar compounds. [sec-lifecycle-pre]

## Pitfalls
Advancing more compounds is not itself better selection. [sec-lifecycle-options] Traditional development also passes information forward through estimates, models and safety findings, so the difference is not that one path passes verdicts and the other distributions. [sec-lifecycle-reading]

## Check yourself
- Q: Must a program finish one phase before starting the next? A: No, 21 CFR 312.21 allows overlap. [sec-lifecycle]
- Q: Where is the prior for later phases first built? A: Before Phase I, from animal data, mechanistic models and similar compounds. [sec-lifecycle-pre]
