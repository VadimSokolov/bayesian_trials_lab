## What it is
The calculation supposes approval moved earlier by the all-trial median saving and that N patients received the treatment during that window, at the trial's placebo event rate of about 2.4 percent a year and its relative reduction of 20 percent. [21/timeline-and-the-acceleration-bound]
The events averted are N times that rate times that reduction times the years saved. [21/timeline-and-the-acceleration-bound]

## Why it matters
Two orders of magnitude separate it from the in-trial figures. [21/timeline-and-the-acceleration-bound]
That asymmetry is the real economics of trial design: the value of a trial is overwhelmingly in the decision it enables for the population outside it. [21/timeline-and-the-acceleration-bound]

## In the book
The post-trial population size is the $M$ of the loss function in the working paper. [21/timeline-and-the-acceleration-bound]
For REWIND the all-trial median decision is the end of the trial, so there is no median calendar saving. [21/timeline-and-the-acceleration-bound]
The summary table ranks the population QALYs row last and defends it only as a bound. [21/summary-of-the-accounting]

## Pitfalls
The calculation assumes approval, reimbursement, manufacturing and clinical uptake would all move forward by the full years, and none of those would. [21/timeline-and-the-acceleration-bound]
Manufacturing capacity for these agents was the binding constraint on access, so the realistic acceleration is a fraction of the statistical one. [21/timeline-and-the-acceleration-bound]

## Check yourself
- Q: Is the acceleration bound a forecast? A: No, it is an upper bound under assumptions that did not hold. [21/timeline-and-the-acceleration-bound]
- Q: Which assumption would you relax first? A: The book names approval, reimbursement, manufacturing and uptake as all moving forward by the full time. [21/timeline-and-the-acceleration-bound]
